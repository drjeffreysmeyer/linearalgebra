    Section 2.6 The Subspace Theorem

    Theorem 2.6.1 Subspace Theorem.

    If \(V\) is a vector space over \(F\) and \(W\subset V\) is a subspace, then the vector space operations of \(V\) restricted to \(W\) make \(W\) a vector space (i.e. \(W\) satisfies axioms (VS1)-(VS8)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Hint.
    Try a direct proof. For as important and useful as the Subspace Theorem is, its proof is surprisingly straightforward. You will need to verify each property (VS1)-(VS8). Most properties are immediately inherited from \(V\text{.}\) The only two that are not as immediate are (V2) and inverses (V3) You may find Proposition 2.4.2, Proposition 2.2.4, and Definition 2.4.1 useful.
    🔗
    🔗
    The Subspace Theorem is very powerful for proving things are vector spaces quickly without having to verify (VS1)-(VS8). In particular, together with Proposition 2.3.2 and Proposition 2.3.6, we can construct and verify infinitely many new vector spaces.
    🔗

    Example 2.6.2 Examples of Subspaces from Calculus.

    If \(I\subset \R\) is an interval (possibly all of \(\R\)), then in your calculus courses you showed that each of the following subsets of the vector space \(\mathcal{F}(I,\R)\) satisfy (S0)-(S2), or in other words, is a subspace:
    🔗
    1. Continuous Functions:
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{C}(I,\R) = \{ f\in \mathcal{F}(I,\R) \ | \ f \text{ is continuous at each point in the domain } I\}, \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Differentiable Functions:
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{D}(I,\R) = \{ f\in \mathcal{F}(I,\R) \ | \ f \text{ is differentiable at each point in the domain } I\}, \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Integrable functions:
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{M}(I,\R) = \{ f\in \mathcal{F}(I,\R) \ | \ \int_I f(x)dx < \infty\}, \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Polynomials:
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{P}(\R) = \{ f\in \mathcal{F}(I,\R) \ | \ f \text{ is a polynomial}\}, \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Polynomials of bounded degree:
      \begin{equation*} \mathcal{P}_n(\R) = \{ f\in \mathcal{F}(I,\R) \ | \ f \text{ is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to } n\}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    The Subspace Theorem then implies these are vector spaces.
    🔗
    🔗
    These and related vector spaces play important roles throughout mathematics, physics, and computer science, including in functional analysis, Fourier analysis, wave mechanics, quantum mechanics, centrality, and ranking.
    🔗

    Discussion 2.6.3.

    In Fourier analysis we look at the set of functions \(f:\R\to \R\) that repeat every \(2\pi\) (i.e. \(f(x+2\pi)=f(x)\)). Let \(\mathcal{P}_{2\pi}(\R,\R)\) denote this set. How you might show this collection of functions is a vector space? Explain your thinking at each step, why you make the arguments you do.
